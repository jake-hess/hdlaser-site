Three rules, and that’s the whole deal

$180k

Your baseline is protected

The first $180,000 of revenue every year is yours, entirely. Jake earns nothing on it. That number is what you are finishing this year at, so day one of the deal changes nothing about your income.

30%

Jake is paid on growth only

On revenue above the baseline, Jake earns 30% of the net profit. You keep the other 70%. If the growth doesn’t come, Jake earned nothing and you lost nothing.

30 → 15%

It steps down in four stages

Once revenue holds above $1,000,000, Jake’s share steps down as he hands off: 25% when the $1M holds, 20% when a manager runs the floor, 15% when Jake has stepped out and a team runs it. If revenue falls back under $1M, Jake steps back in and the 30% returns until it’s rebuilt.

Owner and operator are two different jobs

Right now you are both. The plan is to make HD Laser run without either of us standing at the machine.

The operator

  • Works in the business: quotes, cuts, engraves, opens the door, answers the phone.
  • Income stops the day they stop. A vacation costs a week of revenue.
  • Judged by how hard they worked this week.
  • This is what you have been for ten years, and it is why the shop survived.

The owner

  • Works on the business: systems, people, brand, pricing, where the next thousand customers come from.
  • Income continues whether or not they are in the room, because other people and systems do the operating.
  • Judged by what the business is worth and what it earns without them.
  • This is where the deal is trying to put you, with the shop still yours.

Letting go of operating is not letting go of owning. It is the only way ownership starts to pay.

How the company grows up, one stage at a time

Each stage is a milestone you can see on the dashboards, not a date on a calendar. Jake’s share only steps down when the milestone is real.

30%

Build (now to $1M)

Jake runs growth: sales, marketing, systems, the website and dashboards you already have, hiring the first people who can quote and produce without you. You keep 100% of the baseline and 70% of the growth profit.

25%

Hold ($1M held for two quarters)

Revenue has stayed above $1M for two quarters in a row. Jake still runs growth, but the first step-down happens here because the shop has proven it can carry the size. You keep 75% of the growth profit.

20%

Hand off (a manager runs the floor)

A floor manager owns the daily open, the queue and the close, using the checklists and the staff portal. Jake is out of daily operations and leads only sales and marketing. Part of the step-down funds the manager. You keep 80%.

15%

Run (Jake stepped out)

A small team runs it and the dashboards have stayed green for two quarters without Jake in the building. His share settles at 15%. The rest of the room he gave up goes to the people who run it, who will need a stake or a bonus pool to stay. You are an owner now, not the operator, and you keep 85%.

↻

Rebuild, if needed

If revenue falls back under $1M at any stage, Jake comes back in, the share returns to 30%, and it stays there until the shop is back above $1M for two quarters. Then the ladder starts again from 25%. You are never left holding a bigger company alone.

What protects each of you

What protects Hugh

  • The baseline. Your current income is never in the split.
  • No fixed cost. Jake is not a salary. No growth, no check.
  • You keep 70 cents of every new profit dollar, rising to 75, 80 and 85 cents as Jake steps down.
  • One set of books. Both of you read the same money page, the same P&L, the same ledger. Nothing is calculated in someone’s head.
  • The shop stays yours. This is a share of profit, not a share of the company.
  • Either of you can end it with 60 days’ notice. Jake’s share applies only to revenue booked before the end date.

What protects Jake

  • The definition of net profit is written down: revenue, minus cost of goods, minus operating costs, before owner draws and before Jake’s share. Agreed once, checked by a CPA.
  • Monthly statement, quarterly settlement, from the money page.
  • Each step-down happens only when its milestone is real and visible on the dashboards: $1M held two quarters, a manager running the floor, a team running it without him.
  • If revenue slips under $1M, the 30% comes back. Jake’s time is protected the same way your baseline is.
  • Growth spend (ads, hires, tools) is agreed before it’s spent and comes out before profit is split, so nobody is paid on money that was reinvested.

Questions worth asking before you say yes

  • What exactly counts as net profit? Write the formula on one line and have your CPA initial it.
  • What happens to the baseline next year? Proposal: reset it each January to the prior year’s revenue, so Jake is always paid on new growth, never twice on the same growth.
  • What if a big customer leaves? The split is on actual profit, so a bad year pays Jake less automatically. Nothing is owed on a forecast.
  • Who decides on spending? Anything over an agreed amount needs both of you. It comes off the top before the split.
  • What does each milestone mean in writing? $1M held: two consecutive quarters above $250,000. Manager runs the floor: a named person owns open, queue and close for a full quarter. Stepped out: Jake not in daily operations and dashboards green for two quarters.
  • What about the people who run it later? The 20% and 15% stages set aside part of the profit for the manager and the team. That comes from Jake’s step-down first, not from your baseline.
