Owner and operator are two different jobs

Right now you are both. The plan is to make HD Laser run without either of us standing at the machine.

The operator

  • Works in the business: quotes, cuts, engraves, opens the door, answers the phone.
  • Income stops the day they stop. A vacation costs a week of revenue.
  • Judged by how hard they worked this week.
  • This is what you have been for ten years, and it is why the shop survived.

The owner

  • Works on the business: systems, people, brand, pricing, where the next thousand customers come from.
  • Income continues whether or not they are in the room, because other people and systems do the operating.
  • Judged by what the business is worth and what it earns without them.
  • This is where the deal is trying to put you, with the shop still yours.

Letting go of operating is not letting go of owning. It is the only way ownership starts to pay.

Rebuild, if needed

If revenue falls back under $1M at any stage, Jake comes back in, the share returns to 30%, and it stays there until the shop is back above $1M for two quarters. Then the ladder starts again from 25%. You are never left holding a bigger company alone.

When Jake’s share comes down, it is bought, not lost

Jake is building something that will be worth more at every step. If he simply handed points back, he would be paid for the work and nothing for the build. So each 5-point step is a buy-back: the points return to your cap table at a price, valued at a multiple of EBITDA, the same way a buyer or a strategic partner would value the whole company. That is what makes the points worth something later.

Four times EBITDA. Small profitable service businesses change hands in the 3 to 5× range; 4× is the middle. The same multiple values the whole company, so the points are priced exactly as a buyer would price them.

Paid from the profit those points now earn you. At 48 months and 4×, the points pay for themselves: nothing comes out of your pocket.

The envelope rules

  • One envelope per department, reset on the 1st. Empty means wait, not borrow.
  • Custom orders take a deposit that covers the material before anything is cut.
  • Every Monday, both of you look at the same page: what each envelope has left.
  • Unspent materials and labor roll into the cash reserve, not into the split.
  • Anything over an envelope needs both signatures and a note in the ledger saying why.

Why lead times are the release valve

  • A longer lead time costs nothing. Overspending costs the split, the reserve, and eventually the business.
  • Customers accept “three weeks” far better than they accept a rushed job or a shop that closed.
  • When the materials envelope is empty by the 20th, that is a signal to raise prices or the envelope, decided together at month end, not a reason to spend past it.
  • The percentages are starting points. The money page shows the real share every month; adjust them quarterly.

The words, in plain English

So nothing on this page, or in the conversations after it, needs translating.

  • Revenue. Everything customers paid you. The top line. Sales tax collected is not revenue.
  • COGS (cost of goods). What it cost to make what you sold: blanks, tumblers, wood, packaging, the labor on the job.
  • Gross profit. Revenue minus COGS. What is left to run the shop with.
  • G&A (general and administrative). The cost of existing: rent, insurance, software, bookkeeping, utilities. It does not change when you sell one more cup, which is why it shrinks as a share when revenue grows.
  • Operating costs. G&A plus marketing plus any labor not on a specific job.
  • Net profit. Revenue minus COGS minus operating costs. The number this deal splits, on growth only.
  • EBITDA. Net profit before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. The cleanest read on whether the shop itself makes money. The $30,000 hole is measured here, the calculator shows it at every size, and the buy-back multiple is applied to it.
  • Margin. Profit as a share of revenue. 30% margin means 30 cents of every dollar is profit.
  • Baseline. The $180,000 of revenue that stays entirely yours. The line the split starts above.
  • Owner draw. Money you take out of the business for yourself. Not an expense of the business; it comes after net profit.
  • Cap table. Who owns what share of a company. Yours has one name on it and this deal does not change that. It matters later, when the people who run the shop need a stake to stay.
  • Multiple. A price expressed as years of EBITDA. 4× means paying four years of EBITDA to own something outright. It is how buyers value a business, and how this deal values the points.
  • Buy-back. Paying for points that come off Jake’s share instead of taking them for free. They return to your cap table paid for.
  • Owner vs operator. The operator does the work. The owner owns the thing that does the work. The whole plan is moving you from the first to the second without giving anything up.

Questions worth asking before you say yes

  • What exactly counts as net profit? Write the formula on one line and have your CPA initial it.
  • What happens to the baseline next year? Proposal: reset it each January to the prior year’s revenue, so Jake is always paid on new growth, never twice on the same growth.
  • What if a big customer leaves? The split is on actual profit, so a bad year pays Jake less automatically. Nothing is owed on a forecast.
  • Who decides on spending? The marketing rate and ceiling are agreed up front and Jake runs the envelope. Anything else over an agreed amount needs both of you. All of it comes off the top before the split.
  • Where does the $30,000 figure come from? The money page. Confirm it together with the CPA before signing, and write the exact number on the page.
  • What does each milestone mean in writing? $1M held: two consecutive quarters above $250,000. Manager runs the floor: a named person owns open, queue and close for a full quarter. Stepped out: Jake not in daily operations and dashboards green for two quarters.
  • How is a buy-back priced, exactly? Points released × trailing-twelve-month EBITDA before the split × the multiple. Written on the page with a worked example so there is nothing to argue about later.
  • What about the people who run it later? The 20% and 15% stages set aside part of the profit for the manager and the team. That comes from Jake’s step-down first, not from your baseline.
